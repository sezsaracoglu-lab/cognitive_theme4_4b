The classification database: how knowledge is stored, and what makes a thing what it is. Prepared for Sezen.

A note from your professor

Dear Sezen, look at the photo that opens this section: a Great Dane, a Jack Russell, a Dalmatian puppy, a cat and a rabbit. In under a second you say “dogs, cat, rabbit”, even though the black-and-white colouring would put the Dalmatian next to the cat. How does your mind know that three animals that look so different are the same kind of thing?

These ten pages answer two questions. First, how is knowledge stored and retrieved? (semantic networks and spreading activation). Second, what gives a category its structure? Here you meet four theories in turn: classical, prototype, exemplar and essentialist. Each new theory fixes the problems of the one before and brings its own. Keep a mental scoreboard as you go. That scoreboard is what exam questions test.

You have a head start: much of this overlaps with Murphy’s Categories and Concepts, which you’ve already studied. Watch for the new parts: semantic networks, the Posner dot-pattern study, and the problems of context, conjunction and economy. How to read: Key Terms first, then the lessons in order. For each one, study the diagram, read the bullets, say the memory hook out loud, and try the Comprehension Check before you open the answers. — Your Professor

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe semantic networks (nodes, links), the category and feature verification tasks, and why speed is the key measure.
  • Explain Collins & Loftus’s (1975) spreading activation model and how it explains semantic priming. Contrast semantic networks with neural networks.
  • Name the functions of concepts: understanding, prediction, learning and communication.
  • Distinguish natural kinds, artifacts, ad hoc and metaphorical categories along Goldstone & Kersten’s (2003) similarity scale.
  • Explain the classical view and its three problems: defining features, graded structure/typicality, and fuzzy boundaries.
  • Explain the prototype approach, the Posner et al. (1967) dot-pattern study, and the prototype’s problems (correlated properties, context, conjunctions).
  • Explain the exemplar approach, what it solves, its problems (Posner, economy), and Malt’s (1989) “both” conclusion.
  • Explain the essentialist approach (concepts as theories; top-down) and Rips’s (1989) challenge to similarity-based views.

📚 Key Terms: read these first

TermPlain-language definitionExample
Semantic networkA model of knowledge as an associative network: concepts are nodes, and relationships between them are links.canary — bird — animal
Node / linkA node stands for one specific concept; a link is a relationship between two concepts.“canary” linked to “pet”
Category verification taskVerify simple category statements as fast as possible.“A robin is a bird”
Feature verification taskVerify statements about features of a concept as fast as possible.“A cat has pointy ears”
Neural networkA model where nodes are like neurons and knowledge is distributed; no single node means “canary”.connectionist models
Spreading activation modelCollins & Loftus (1975): activating one node spreads activation along links to related nodes.yellow → lemon, sun
ThresholdThe level of summed activation a node must reach before the concept is activated or identified.“lemon” recognised
Semantic primingProcessing one stimulus speeds up processing of a related stimulus.“yellow” speeds “lemon”
Natural kindsAlso called natural categories: occur naturally in the world; members share basic features.flower, bird
ArtifactsAlso called artifact categories: designed by humans for functions; members share fewer features.tools, sports, furniture
Ad hoc categoryFormed “on the fly” to serve a goal (Barsalou, 1983).things to take on vacation
Metaphorical conceptVery different situations united by an underlying theme.an “emotional prison”
Similarity-based approachesCategorise by judging similarity between the target and a standard in long-term memory.classical, prototype, exemplar
Classical viewMembers share features that are both necessary and sufficient (Bruner, Goodnow & Austin, 1956).triangle
Graded structureCategory members are not equal; some are better examples than others.table vs rug as furniture
Typicality effectSome members are judged more typical, and people agree strongly about which ones.robin > penguin
Fuzzy boundariesCategories have no clear-cut edges.is bowling a sport?
Prototype approachMembers are features-likely-to-be-present; categorise by similarity to the prototype.robin as the bird prototype
Family resemblanceSharing many features with other members of the category.robin: flies, chirps, feathers, beak
Prototype(1) the abstracted “average” standard; (2) the most representative member(s).“bird” average
Exemplar approachCategories are represented by stored examples, with no abstraction.every guitar you’ve met
Correlated propertiesFeatures that go together within a category.small birds sing, large birds squawk
Economy problemStoring every single encounter seems implausible.Komatsu (1992)
Essentialist approachConcepts as theories: categorisation is based on a belief about the concept’s core essence (top-down).Medin & Ortony (1989)
Bottom-up vs top-downBottom-up: build up from perceived features. Top-down: start from general knowledge and ideas.similarity vs essence

📎 Also on p. 198: the end of the face-recognition summary

The scan starts with the last lines of the previous section. Faces may be “special” stimuli with a dedicated brain module; others see face recognition as a case of perceptual expertise. Faces show strong configural effects. Females tend to be better at facial identification than males, and people recognise same-culture faces more readily than different-culture faces. Recognising your own face relies more on the right hemisphere. Names are the hardest thing to retrieve: there is a gradient from recognising that a face is familiar (easiest) to retrieving the name (hardest). Name-retrieval models propose either serial or parallel processing of person information.
